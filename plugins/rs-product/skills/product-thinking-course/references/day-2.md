## 5. Day 2 — Interrogate the why (65 min)

### Concept (12 min)

Clients tell you what they want. The next job is finding what that want rests on.

Most feature requests trace back to one of five roots:

1. A real pattern in their data
2. One loud customer
3. A competitor's screenshot
4. An executive's opinion
5. A conference talk

Only the first is evidence. The other four are untested beliefs.

**The ladder.** For each requested feature, ask what makes them believe that, until you reach something pointable — a ticket, a number, a recording, a named person — or you reach nothing. Reaching nothing is a finding, and you flag it to the client rather than building on it quietly.

**Motivation.** Under every real pattern is a person with a reason to act or not act. People don't abandon a prescription out of laziness. They abandon it because of a cost surprise, or embarrassment, or a 20-minute drive that turned out to be wasted. The jobs-to-be-done framing: people hire a product to make progress in a situation, and fire it when it stops helping. Ask what happened immediately before, and what they did instead.°

**Write findings as needs, not features.** "I need to know the price before I drive over" can be solved five ways. "A price notification" can be built one way. Staying at the need level keeps the options open one more day.✓

### Quiz — Day 2

**Easy 1.** Which is an opportunity rather than a solution?
- A) I need push notifications when my refill is ready
- B) I need to know my prescription is ready without driving there
- C) We need an SMS integration
- D) I need a better app

→ **B.** A names the mechanism. D is an adjective.

**Easy 2.** Client: *"Our sales team says customers want integrations."*
- A) Which integrations?
- B) Can I talk to the rep who heard it, or see the call notes?
- C) How many customers?
- D) Which competitor has integrations?

→ **B.** Goes to the source. A gets a list with nothing behind it.

**Easy 3.** A stakeholder's why bottoms out at *"our CEO saw it at a conference."*
- A) Drop the feature
- B) Build it, the CEO decides
- C) Record the root as opinion, flag it, propose the cheapest test
- D) Ask the CEO directly

→ **C.** Record the confidence level and reduce the cost of being wrong.

**Medium 4.** You have support tickets but no analytics. A lead says the tickets prove the problem. Strongest objection?
- A) Tickets are anecdotal
- B) Tickets capture only the people who cared enough to complain; the silent majority may have a different problem
- C) Tickets are old
- D) Support staff summarize inaccurately

→ **B.** Names the specific bias. A is true and generic.

**Medium 5.** Dana says *"customers want to feel rewarded."* You want to test it cheaply.
- A) Has anyone ever asked you for a rewards program, in writing?
- B) What would rewarded feel like?
- C) Should we survey customers?
- D) What's the budget for rewards?

→ **A.** Tests whether there is a single instance behind the claim. If the answer is no, you've found the gap in one question.

**Medium 6.** Four abandonment causes in the tickets. Which question best decides where to start?
- A) Which is most common?
- B) Which is most common and cheapest for us to remove?
- C) Which annoys the pharmacists most?
- D) Which does Dana care about?

→ **B.** Frequency alone can pick a problem you can't solve. Volume against tractability picks a first move.

**Hard 7.** *(Free text)* A client insists a feature is needed "because we lost a deal over it." Write the three questions you'd ask and what each rules out.

*Rubric (10 pts):* Asks how many deals, not just the one (3). Asks whether the feature was the stated reason or the real reason — losing parties give polite reasons (4). Asks whether the lost deal was in their target segment (2). Each exclusion named (1).

**Hard 8.** *(Free text)* You've interrogated all 6 requested features. Four rest on nothing. Write the opening two sentences of how you'd tell the client, who signed off on the list and has a boss.

*Rubric (10 pts):* Avoids "wrong," "no," "actually" (2). Leads with their outcome rather than your finding (3). Frames the gap as risk to their goal, not error on their part (3). Offers a next step (2).

## Rules to reveal (only after the artifact is graded)

From RightShift Product Rules, sections 1 and 9.

- Build to find the bottleneck. Don't predict it.
- Don't build the app because everyone has an app.
- Acknowledge a miss. Don't argue it.
- Push back with facts and acknowledge in the same message.
- Say what you don't know.

The last three are the answer to Hard 8. Grade Hard 8 against them: the strongest response leads with the client's outcome, states the gap as risk to that outcome, and does not defend.
